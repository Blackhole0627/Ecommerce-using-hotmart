What the Kegel Method Is, and Where It Came From

Before you learn any technique, it helps to understand where it actually comes from — because that changes how you think about it.

This isn't a trend, a secret, or a wellness fad. It's the direct descendant of a medical protocol that has been studied, refined, and used for more than 75 years.

In 1948, American gynecologist Dr. Arnold Kegel published a method for strengthening the pelvic floor in women recovering from childbirth-related incontinence. At the time, surgery was the default treatment. Kegel proposed something radically simpler: teach women to actively and voluntarily strengthen the same muscle group that supports the bladder, uterus, and rectum.

THE SQUEEZE METHOD

The results were striking enough to change clinical practice. A large share of his patients saw meaningful improvement using nothing but repeated, correctly performed contractions — no surgery required. To this day, this category of exercise carries his name.

Since then, decades of further research have confirmed and expanded on his original findings. Pelvic floor muscle training is now considered first-line therapy for stress urinary incontinence by urogynecologists worldwide, specifically because it is effective and carries very little risk when done correctly.

What you're about to learn isn't a wellness trend. It's the modern, refined version of a medical approach that has been helping women for over seven decades.

Understanding Your Pelvic Floor

You don't need a medical degree to train effectively — but understanding what you're actually working with changes everything about how well you do it.

What the pelvic floor actually is

The pelvic floor (sometimes called the pelvic diaphragm) is a group of muscles and connective tissue that stretches like a hammock across the base of your pelvis — from your pubic bone in front to your tailbone in back. The primary muscle involved in voluntary contraction is the pubococcygeus, often shortened to "PC muscle," which is the main target of the exercises in this book.

What it supports

This hammock of muscle holds up three major structures:

  • The bladder, at the front
  • The uterus, in the middle
  • The rectum, at the back
The pelvic floor cradles these organs from below.
FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

Because all three sit on the same muscular support, a weakened pelvic floor rarely causes just one isolated problem. Bladder control, pelvic organ support, and bowel function are all connected to the strength of this same muscle group — which is why improving it tends to create benefits across several areas at once, not just one.

Its three core functions

  • Support — keeping pelvic organs correctly positioned, resisting the downward pressure of gravity, pregnancy, and aging
  • Sphincter control — allowing you to voluntarily hold or release urine and stool
  • Sexual function — contributing to sensation during penetration and the physical contractions involved in orgasm

Why it weakens

Like every other muscle in your body, the pelvic floor can lose tone and strength. The most common contributors include:

  • Physical inactivity — an unused muscle gradually loses tone, the pelvic floor is no exception
  • Pregnancy and vaginal delivery — from sustained weight-bearing and the mechanical stretch of childbirth
  • Aging and hormonal changes — particularly the drop in estrogen around menopause, which affects tissue elasticity
  • Chronic straining — repeated heavy lifting, chronic constipation, or persistent coughing all place repeated downward pressure on the pelvic floor

The reassuring part is the same as with any other muscle: a weakened pelvic floor responds to training. That is the entire premise of everything in this book.

What the research shows

Pelvic floor muscle training is recognized by urogynecologists as first-line, evidence-based therapy for stress urinary incontinence, precisely because studies have repeatedly shown meaningful symptom improvement with very low risk. Unlike medication or surgery, correctly performed pelvic floor exercise has essentially no negative side effects when done properly.

Consistent training has been associated with:

  • Reduced frequency of urine leakage during coughing, sneezing, laughing, or exercise
  • Better pelvic organ support, which can help prevent or slow the progression of pelvic organ prolapse
  • Improved recovery of pelvic floor strength following vaginal childbirth
  • Better bowel regularity in some women, due to improved coordination of the surrounding musculature
  • A measurable improvement in quality of life scores among women with urinary incontinence, according to multiple systematic reviews

Understanding Urinary Incontinence

If bladder leaks are part of why you picked up this book, you are far from alone — and understanding exactly what type of incontinence you're dealing with helps you understand why pelvic floor training works the way it does.

Stress incontinence

This is leakage triggered by physical pressure on the bladder — coughing, sneezing, laughing, jumping, or lifting something heavy. It happens when the muscles supporting the urethra are too weak to withstand a sudden increase in abdominal pressure. This is the type of incontinence pelvic floor training is most directly and effectively aimed at, because it targets the exact muscles responsible for that support.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

Urge incontinence

This shows up as a sudden, intense need to urinate that arrives quickly, sometimes with leakage before you can reach a bathroom. It's often linked to an overactive bladder, where the bladder muscle contracts at the wrong time. Pelvic floor training can still help here — a strong, well-coordinated pelvic floor can help suppress those urgent contractions — but it is often combined with bladder training techniques for best results.

Mixed incontinence

Many women don't experience a "pure" version of either type — they have a combination of both stress and urge symptoms. This is actually the most common presentation among women, particularly around and after menopause. The good news is that pelvic floor training remains a foundational part of treatment for the mixed presentation as well.

When to see a doctor

Pelvic floor training is safe and effective for the vast majority of women, but certain situations deserve a conversation with a healthcare provider before or alongside starting a program: sudden onset of symptoms, blood in urine, pain during urination, or incontinence following a specific injury or surgery. This program is educational and is not a substitute for a medical evaluation.

In short
  • Stress: leaks from pressure — coughing, laughing, lifting.
  • Urge: a sudden need you cannot delay. Mixed is the most common of all.
  • Sudden onset, blood, or pain deserve a doctor before you train.

Finding the Right Muscle

This is, without exaggeration, the single most important chapter in this book.

Every workout in this program is worthless if it's training the wrong muscle — and research suggests this happens far more often than most women realize.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

A surprising statistic

In a frequently cited clinical study, less than half of women were able to correctly perform a pelvic floor contraction using only verbal or written instructions. More recent estimates suggest as many as one in two women perform Kegel exercises incorrectly on their first attempts — most often by mistakenly engaging the abdominal, buttock, or thigh muscles instead.

This isn't a failure of willpower or intelligence. It's simply a muscle that almost nobody is ever taught to consciously feel, let alone control.

How to identify it correctly

Sitting or lying down, contract as if you were trying to stop the flow of urine, while also imagining you're holding in gas. The muscle that lifts up and inward is your pelvic floor.

Sign you've got it right: a subtle internal "lifting" sensation, without tightening your stomach, buttocks, or thighs.

Sign you've got it wrong: holding your breath, or feeling your stomach harden at the same time.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

⚠️ One-time confirmation test only (not a daily exercise): the next time you urinate, stop the stream for 1-2 seconds, just once, purely to confirm you're engaging the right muscle. Making this a regular habit can interfere with normal bladder function.

Why lifting matters more than squeezing

A subtle but important detail: correct technique involves squeezing and lifting the pelvic floor upward and inward — not bearing down or straining, which is a common and counterproductive mistake covered in more detail in the next chapter.

In short
  • Lift up and inward, as if stopping urine and holding in gas.
  • Your stomach, thighs and buttocks stay completely relaxed.
  • As many as one in two women get this wrong at first — this chapter is worth your time.

The Science of Two Muscle Types

Here's a detail almost no beginner guide mentions, and it directly explains why this program trains two very different types of contractions instead of just one.

Two fiber types, one muscle group

Anatomical research shows the pelvic floor is made up of a mix of two distinct types of muscle fiber: roughly two-thirds are Type I (slow-twitch) fibers, and about one-third are Type II (fast-twitch) fibers. Each type has a completely different job.

Type I — slow-twitch, endurance fibers

These fibers are fatigue-resistant and built for sustained, low-level effort. They're responsible for maintaining baseline tone in the pelvic floor around the clock — quietly holding your pelvic organs in place while you sit, stand, and walk through an ordinary day, without you ever consciously thinking about it.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

Type II — fast-twitch, power fibers

These fibers generate much greater force very quickly, but fatigue far faster. They're recruited during sudden spikes in abdominal pressure — the exact moment you cough, sneeze, laugh hard, or lift something heavy. This is your pelvic floor's emergency response system, and it needs to fire instantly to prevent a leak in that split second.

Why this matters for how you train

A training program that only builds slow, sustained holds will strengthen your baseline tone, but will do very little for that fast emergency response. A program that only trains fast pulses builds quick reflexes, but neglects the endurance that keeps everything supported for hours at a time. This is precisely why this program pairs both: Strength-style holds to build Type I endurance, and Pulse-style rapid contractions to build Type II power — the same dual approach used in modern pelvic floor physical therapy protocols.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

Your pelvic floor isn't one simple muscle doing one simple job. It's a coordinated team of two different fiber types — and real training respects both halves of that team.

In short
  • Type I fibres (about two-thirds) hold steady tone all day long.
  • Type II fibres fire fast to stop a leak the instant you cough or lift.
  • Good training uses both: slow holds and quick pulses.

The 5 Most Common Mistakes

Understanding these mistakes ahead of time will save you weeks of wasted effort — and in some cases, prevent you from making symptoms worse instead of better.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

Mistake 1 — Bearing down instead of lifting

Some women unconsciously push or strain downward, the same motion used during a bowel movement, instead of lifting the muscle up and in. Over time, this can actually weaken the pelvic floor further rather than strengthening it. If you feel pressure moving downward and outward, you're doing the opposite of what you want.

Mistake 2 — Engaging the wrong muscles

As covered in Chapter 5, it's extremely common to substitute abdominal, buttock, or inner thigh contractions for a true pelvic floor contraction. If your belly hardens, your glutes lift you slightly off the seat, or your thighs squeeze together, you're very likely missing the target muscle entirely.

Mistake 3 — Holding your breath

Instinctively holding your breath during a contraction raises pressure in your abdomen and shifts effort toward your core muscles instead of your pelvic floor. Breathing steadily throughout each contraction — covered in the next chapter — is not optional, it's part of correct technique.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

Mistake 4 — Skipping the release

Many women focus so intensely on the squeeze that they rush through, or entirely skip, a full release between repetitions. A muscle that never fully relaxes can't generate a strong contraction on the next rep, and over time this can contribute to a chronically tense pelvic floor — which carries its own set of uncomfortable symptoms.

Mistake 5 — Overtraining

More is not automatically better. Doing hundreds of repetitions a day, or training every single day without any rest, can fatigue the pelvic floor the same way overtraining fatigues any other muscle group — leading to soreness, increased urgency, or even pain. Research-backed guidelines generally recommend a moderate number of sets and repetitions performed most days of the week, with built-in rest, rather than constant maximal effort.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

If you only remember one thing from this chapter: the goal is a muscle that can both contract strongly and relax completely. Training only one half of that equation will hold back your results.

In short
  • Bearing down instead of lifting, or squeezing the wrong muscles.
  • Holding your breath, or skipping the release between repetitions.
  • Overtraining — with this muscle, more is not better.

Breathing: The Forgotten Foundation

Almost nobody talks about this, but breathing largely determines whether you're activating the right muscle or quietly compensating with tension somewhere else.

The simple rule

  • Inhale as you release the muscle
  • Exhale as you contract the muscle

Holding your breath during a contraction is one of the most common mistakes there is — and it's the reason many women feel like "nothing is happening," when in reality they're simply activating the wrong muscle group because of breath-holding.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

The diaphragm connection

Your diaphragm and your pelvic floor move together as a coordinated unit. As you inhale, your diaphragm moves downward and your pelvic floor naturally relaxes and descends slightly. As you exhale, your diaphragm rises and your pelvic floor naturally lifts. Working with this rhythm — rather than against it — makes every contraction more effective and far less effortful.

In short
  • Inhale as you release. Exhale as you contract.
  • Your diaphragm and pelvic floor move together as one unit.
  • Breath-holding is why many women feel that nothing is happening.

Relaxation: Letting Go Is Also Training

There's a common belief that "tighter is always better" — this is false, and acting on it can actually cause problems rather than solve them.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

A pelvic floor that stays tense all the time, without ever fully relaxing, can lead to discomfort, pain, and in some cases the very same symptoms — urgency, incomplete emptying — that women are often trying to train away in the first place. This is a real, recognized condition sometimes called an overactive or hypertonic pelvic floor.

A simple relaxation exercise

After each set of contractions, pause for 10 to 15 seconds and consciously notice the sensation of fully letting go — as though the muscle is melting or softening completely. If this is hard to feel at first, that's completely normal. Voluntary relaxation is a skill, just like contraction is, and it develops with practice.

Signs your pelvic floor may be overly tense

  • A frequent sense of urgency, even shortly after emptying your bladder
  • Discomfort or pain during intercourse
  • A general sense of tightness or pressure in the pelvic region, especially after long periods of sitting
FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

If any of these sound familiar, it's worth mentioning to a healthcare provider or pelvic floor physical therapist — a hypertonic pelvic floor sometimes needs a different approach than simple strengthening, and this book's relaxation practice is a helpful complement to, not a replacement for, that kind of evaluation.

A pelvic floor that can't relax is just as much of a problem as one that can't contract. Training both halves of that equation is what this program is built around.

In short
  • Tighter is not better — a permanently tense pelvic floor causes its own symptoms.
  • Pause 10 to 15 seconds after each set and let go completely.
  • Constant urgency, tightness or pain during sex deserves a professional opinion.

Your Pelvic Floor Through Every Life Stage

Pelvic floor needs aren't static — they shift at specific points in a woman's life.

Understanding your current stage helps set the right expectations.

Postpartum

Vaginal delivery in particular places significant mechanical stress on the pelvic floor, and some degree of weakness afterward is extremely common — not a sign that anything went wrong. Most guidance recommends waiting for medical clearance (typically around six weeks postpartum) before beginning structured pelvic floor training, and starting gently from there. Even women who deliver via cesarean section can benefit from pelvic floor training, since pregnancy itself places sustained weight and pressure on these muscles for many months regardless of delivery method.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

Perimenopause and menopause

Declining estrogen levels during this transition affect the elasticity and blood supply of pelvic tissue, which can contribute to reduced lubrication, thinning tissue, and increased incidence of both stress and urge incontinence. Research indicates pelvic floor training can meaningfully improve urinary symptoms and quality of life during this stage, even though tissue changes related to hormone levels themselves require a broader conversation with a healthcare provider.

Post-hysterectomy

Removal of the uterus changes some of the internal support structure of the pelvis. Pelvic floor training is frequently recommended both before and after this type of surgery specifically to help reorganize and reinforce the remaining support structures.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

General aging

Independent of any of the above, muscle mass and tone naturally decline with age throughout the entire body — the pelvic floor is not an exception. This is, in many ways, the simplest and most universal reason to train it: like any other muscle, it benefits from consistent use at every stage of life, not just during a specific life event.

In short
  • Postpartum: wait for medical clearance, then begin gently.
  • Perimenopause: falling estrogen makes this support matter more, not less.
  • After hysterectomy, and simply with age, training still helps.

This is typically when the earliest noticeable changes appear — often described as a subtle increase in control or a slightly reduced frequency of minor leaks. Myths vs. Facts

Myth: Kegels are only for women who've had children

Fact: Pelvic floor weakness affects women who have never been pregnant too — from general aging, low activity levels, chronic constipation, or high-impact exercise without adequate pelvic floor conditioning. Every woman has a pelvic floor worth training, regardless of pregnancy history.

Myth: More repetitions always means faster results

Fact: As covered in Chapter 7, overtraining can fatigue and even worsen pelvic floor function. Consistent, correctly performed, moderate-volume training outperforms excessive daily repetitions every time.

Myth: If you can't feel anything happening, it isn't working

Fact: In the earliest stage of training, most of what's happening is neurological — your brain learning to reliably find and activate the right muscle — before visible strength gains appear. Absence of a dramatic sensation in week one doesn't mean the process isn't working.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

Myth: You should be able to feel results immediately

Fact: As outlined in Chapter 11, meaningful change typically takes several weeks of consistent practice. This is completely normal and consistent with how every other muscle in the body adapts to new training.

Myth: Pelvic floor exercises are only about bladder control

Fact: While bladder control is one of the most talked-about benefits, this same muscle group is directly tied to pelvic organ support, core stability, and sexual sensation. Training it touches far more of daily life than most women expect going in.

In short
  • Not only for mothers — every woman has a pelvic floor worth training.
  • More repetitions is not faster, and feeling nothing early is normal.
  • It reaches past bladder control: organ support, core stability and sensation.

The Knack: A Real-World Technique

Everything so far has been about building strength over time.

This chapter is about something different: a specific, in-the-moment technique you can use starting today, regardless of what level you're currently training at.

What it is

Pelvic floor physical therapists sometimes refer to this technique as "The Knack." The idea is simple: consciously contract your pelvic floor firmly, right before — not during, before — a moment you know is about to put pressure on your bladder. That means a beat before you cough, sneeze, laugh hard, or lift something heavy.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

Why timing is everything

Recall from Chapter 6 that your fast-twitch fibers are responsible for that split-second emergency response. The Knack essentially trains you to consciously trigger that response on purpose, slightly ahead of time, instead of hoping your body reacts fast enough on its own. Over time, with enough repetition, this pre-emptive contraction can become an automatic reflex — the same way flinching before a loud noise eventually becomes automatic.

How to practice it

  • Notice an upcoming trigger — you feel a sneeze building, or you're about to pick up something heavy
  • A moment before it happens, contract your pelvic floor firmly, the same way you learned in Chapter 5
  • Hold that contraction through the cough, sneeze, laugh, or lift itself
  • Release normally afterward
FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

This is the one technique in this entire book you can use immediately, in real life, without waiting for weeks of training to see the benefit.

In short
  • Contract firmly just before you cough, sneeze, laugh or lift.
  • Hold through the moment itself, then release normally.
  • With repetition it becomes an automatic reflex — and you can use it today.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long before I notice any difference?

As covered in Chapter 11, most women begin noticing subtle changes around three to six weeks of consistent practice, with more meaningful changes typically appearing by two to three months. Everyone's timeline varies slightly.

Can I do these exercises during my period?

Yes. There is no medical reason to pause pelvic floor training during menstruation. Some women simply prefer a lighter session on heavier flow days, which is entirely a matter of personal comfort.

Is it normal to not feel anything at first?

Yes, and it's covered in detail in Chapter 5 — many women struggle to feel this muscle clearly in the beginning. That's exactly why this book spends an entire chapter on identification before any structured training begins.

Can I overdo it?

Yes. Chapter 7 covers this directly: excessive daily repetitions without rest can fatigue the pelvic floor and worsen symptoms rather than improve them. More is not automatically better with this muscle group, any more than it is with any other.

FEMIVITA
THE SQUEEZE METHOD

Do I need any equipment to get started?

No. Every technique in this book is designed to be performed using bodyweight and voluntary muscle control alone, in any of three positions: lying down, sitting, or standing.

I had a C-section, not a vaginal delivery — do I still need this?

Yes. As covered in Chapter 10, pregnancy itself — independent of delivery method — places sustained weight and pressure on the pelvic floor for many months. A C-section changes the mechanics of delivery, but it doesn't exempt you from the effects of pregnancy on this muscle group.

Will this replace a pelvic floor physical therapist?

Not necessarily, and it isn't meant to. This book teaches the same foundational principles used in pelvic floor physical therapy, but a licensed physical therapist can provide hands-on assessment, biofeedback, and a personalized treatment plan — especially valuable for more complex or persistent symptoms. Think of this book as a strong, evidence-informed starting point, not a replacement for individualized medical care when it's needed.
